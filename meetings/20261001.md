# Main presentation notes — Automatic QA/QC of tide gauges using AI

**Speakers:** Lindsay Abrams (NOAA NOS CO-OPS) & Thanh-Dung Le (Texas A&M University–Corpus Christi, Conrad Blucher Institute).
Co-authors: Bailey Armos, Matt Conlin, Katerina Glebushko, Laura Rojas-Wiltsee (CO-OPS), Brian Colburn, Philippe Tissot (TAMU-CC).
Slides: [`20261001_NOAA_Abrams_and_Le.pdf`](./20261001_NOAA_Abrams_and_Le.pdf). Funded by NOAA CIROH.

> Dr. Tissot's item on filling gaps in historic data was dropped for lack of time.

## The problem

CO-OPS is the authoritative US source for tides, water levels and currents: **300+ stations** on the US coasts and Great Lakes, most of them in busy seaports, feeding real-time data, predictions and products used for navigation safety and coastal risk.

The current QC chain is: **primary + backup water level sensors** → onsite QC (outlier detection, 6-minute averaging) → *preliminary* data → **manual** processing and verification → *verified* data. The manual stage means reviewing QC flags, removing bad data by hand, and filling the remaining gaps from the backup sensor, a neighbour station, or tide predictions.

The recurring data quality issues are **spikes, flats, drifts and data shifts** — and crucially, the hard part is separating those from **rare but real events** (tsunamis, hurricanes, seiches), which look anomalous but are the data you most want to keep.

## Research objectives

One generalized, lightweight model, usable from the West Coast to the East Coast despite very different geographies, that does two things:

1. **Classify** 6-minute preliminary sensor data as good/bad at high accuracy;
2. **Replace and/or fill** gaps in the resulting time series at high accuracy.

The training signal comes for free from the archive: the database holds both **raw** and **verified** water levels, so the model can learn the discrepancies — and the features that correlate with them — from decades of already-QC'd data.

## Method — JEPA in latent space

Thanh-Dung's framework is a **Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture**: PRIMARY, BACKUP and TIDAL PREDICTION are encoded into a latent space, NOAA's VERIFIED series into a *target* latent space. Distance in latent space serves both tasks at once — far from the target ⇒ flag as bad, decoded prediction ⇒ reconstructed "AI-VERIFIED" water level.

Inputs come in three groups: actual values at time *t*; local dynamics over the last 30 min (`_DIFF1`, `ROLL_STD_*`, `ROLL_RANGE_*`); and shared context (cyclic time, tidal phase, station metadata, lon/lat). The model therefore knows the current value, the recent behaviour of the signal, and where/when it is — enough to learn what a physical signal should look like at that station and tidal phase. Reconstruction blends the two sensor encoders by a **confidence weight from correlation with the astronomical tide** rather than 50/50.

Training data is restricted to **GOLD** records: primary and backup both within a magnitude threshold of verified *and* passing a harmonic-correlation check against the astronomical tide. The correlation test matters — a naive 2 cm threshold on sensor − verified discards windows that are demonstrably fine. 29 stations, 7 regions, train 2007–2016 / validation 2017–2018 / test 2019–2020; filtering keeps ~62% of rows (20.9 M training samples), which is plenty. Noise is injected in latent space during training to expose the model to realistic error modes.

## Results

**Reconstruction** — 11 held-out, never-seen stations across 4 coasts: RMSE **3.5 cm** overall, **1.7 cm** where PRIMARY already followed VERIFIED. Individual cases are convincing: spiky primary with flats recovered from 17 cm to 3.3 cm RMSE; a long backup flat-line correctly down-weighted in favour of the primary; Dauphin Island (spikes + shift + a month-long flat) reconstructed to a few mm.

**Detection** — NOAA flags only 2.0% of the test rows as BAD. Episode-level recall is **262/273 = 95.6%** of fault windows; the 11 misses are Panama City spikes with BACKUP absent and small sustained biases at Dauphin Island and Galveston.

**Cost** — the operationally decisive point. 74,225 parameters, 317 KB checkpoint, CPU-only, < 4 GB RAM. One station-month takes 8.8 s, of which **97% is data preparation** (CSV read + calibration + windowing) and 0.2 s is the network. 57 stations × 12 months ≈ 1.7 h sequentially, fully parallelizable per station. The bottleneck is I/O (~200 MB of CSVs per station), not the model.

## Known limitations and next steps

- **Window granularity.** Flagging works on 12 h gap-free windows because the tidal-correlation feature needs ~10 h to be meaningful; any flag inside the window currently condemns the whole window. Moving to per-6-minute flagging is the next step.
- **Bad PRIMARY + missing/bad BACKUP** is the open hard case, with no solid solution yet. Neighbour-station data is the obvious candidate but fails exactly where it's needed: in Alaska neighbours are far away, and when a gauge freezes, the neighbour is usually frozen too.
- **Non-tidal stations** are not supported: tide predictions are a required input today. Ideally the model should produce an output even when one of the inputs is absent.

Since the talk, a small **cross-entropy head** has been added for point-wise flagging (results obtained the morning of the meeting): AUC ≈ 95%, precision 92.5%, recall 69%, F1 79.

## Operational integration (Lindsay)

The deliberate framing of the project was "how simple can one model be and still work for most stations". Where it already helps:

- it flags the full catalogue of suspect behaviours (spikes, flats, drifts, shifts), **including issues hard to see by eye** — it looks at the data differently than a human reviewer does and prompts "wait, is that station actually fine?";
- it has surfaced long-standing suspicions, e.g. persistent deviations between primary and backup at specific stations;
- it shows good skill at predicting where there is no data, i.e. gap filling.

The target workflow is: automatically detect bad data → remove it → automatically fill the resulting gaps that primary and backup can't cover. But **a human stays in the loop**: CO-OPS is not comfortable publishing AI-derived data without a final human review. Their position is that this is *another statistical method* for identifying suspect data and filling gaps, and it gets reviewed like any of their existing methods. That may change later; for now the final review is a person.

## Discussion

> My microphone was not recorded in the session, so my questions below are reconstructed from my notes; the answers come from the transcript.

**(17') Is there really a second physical station at each site? What happens if you remove the backup entirely — are there plans to operate without it in the future?**
No second station: one station, **two sensors (primary + backup) at the same location**. Reality is messier than the design though — backup sensors go down, some periods have no backup at all, and **partner stations often have a single sensor installed**. So making the model work from one sensor is a genuine requirement, not a corner case; the dual-sensor setup is "a luxury". Lindsay's point: the primary/backup comparison is the most informative feature today, but the *other* features (last-30-minute dynamics, tidal phase, station context) are generic and transferable to any single-sensor dataset — defining which features indicate suspect data is the reusable part of the work.

**(23') Relying on AI for decision making is fine; filling with synthetic data is more delicate. Why do you actually need to fill the gaps?**
A **continuous data set** is a primary CO-OPS goal because every downstream use of the data depends on it — but explicitly *not at the risk of introducing biases*. A gap is not filled unless there is a source believed to fit reasonably without biasing the series. They have discussed **publishing multiple versions**: verified-and-gap-filled alongside raw, or alongside suspect-removed-but-not-filled, so users can pick what suits their use case.

**(26') Would you consider using models to correct and fill in-situ data?**
Current fill hierarchy is **backup → neighbour station → tide predictions** (the latter only where the station is strongly tide-dominated). Model output is *not* used today, but CO-OPS does run an operational hydrodynamic model and the question is actively open: if AI-derived data is going to be introduced, model-derived data should be compared on the same footing. Both options are being re-tested in operations later this year.

**Patrick (18') — does the model provide a confidence or uncertainty measure?** The rationale being that a high-confidence flag may only need one human reviewer, while a low-confidence flag warrants several pairs of eyes. Answer: agreed, and that is exactly the direction taken — see the new classification head above. The reconstruction-vs-sensor mismatch is itself already a usable confidence proxy.
